      01Orientation

      01Exam map and study strategy

      This guide follows the Pharmacy Technician Certification Board’s PTCE outline effective January 2026. Each numbered page is a self-contained study unit. The examples and quiz are original study exercises, not official exam questions or instructions for treating patients.

      Official knowledge domain Exam weight Study pages
      Medications 35% 02–05
      Federal Requirements 18.75% 06–08
      Patient Safety and Quality Assurance 23.75% 09–10
      Order Entry and Processing 22.5% 11–14

      The exam contains 90 multiple-choice questions: 80 scored and 10 unscored, with 110 minutes of test time. The unscored questions are mixed in and unidentified. The passing score is 1,400 on a scaled score range of 1,000–1,600, not a percentage-correct cutoff. See the official PTCE outline and PTCB candidate information.

      How to use these pages

      • First pass: Read pages 02–10, then close the guide and explain the difference between two easily confused concepts: side effect versus allergy, transfer versus refill, or high-alert versus look-alike.
      • Math pass: Do pages 11–14 with pencil and paper. For each problem, write the requested unit, cancel unwanted units, and check whether the magnitude is plausible.
      • Recall pass: Make two-way flashcards (brand → generic and generic → brand). Put class, one typical use, and one safety clue on the back.
      • Final pass: Take page 15 without notes. Label every miss as a knowledge, reading, calculation, or judgment error, then redo it the next day.

      Don’t study from an outdated outline

      PTCB removed alligation and nonsterile compounding content and added Drug Supply Chain Security Act (DSCSA) content. Dilutions, dosage calculations, reconstituted-product stability, and product identification still matter (PTCB exam update; official PTCE outline).

      02–05Medications

      02Medications: heart, blood pressure, blood thinners

      Link each drug to a class and use, then add one clue that would make you stop and ask the pharmacist. The brand names below are recognition examples, not a guarantee of availability. Use the National Library of Medicine’s DailyMed to look up current product labeling.

      Generic (brand example) Class and typical use Recognition or safety clue
      Lisinopril (Zestril) ACE inhibitor; hypertension, heart failure Dry cough; lip or tongue swelling may be angioedema
      Losartan (Cozaar) ARB; hypertension Potassium can rise; flag potassium supplements for review
      Amlodipine (Norvasc) Dihydropyridine calcium-channel blocker; hypertension, angina Ankle swelling
      Metoprolol tartrate (Lopressor) / succinate ER (Toprol-XL) Beta blocker; uses vary by formulation Check salt and release form; slow heart rate
      Hydrochlorothiazide (Microzide) Thiazide diuretic; hypertension Electrolyte effects, including low potassium
      Furosemide (Lasix) Loop diuretic; edema Increased urination, low potassium
      Spironolactone (Aldactone) Potassium-sparing diuretic; heart failure, other uses High potassium rather than low potassium
      Atorvastatin (Lipitor) Statin; LDL reduction Unexplained muscle pain warrants review
      Nitroglycerin SL (Nitrostat) Nitrate; angina Dangerous blood-pressure drop with sildenafil or similar drugs
      Warfarin (Jantoven) Vitamin K antagonist anticoagulant Bleeding risk; INR monitoring
      Apixaban (Eliquis) / rivaroxaban (Xarelto) Direct factor Xa inhibitors; anticoagulation Bleeding risk; not interchangeable by name or mg
      Clopidogrel (Plavix) Antiplatelet; prevention of thrombotic events Not the same class as warfarin or apixaban

      Recognition patterns and a common trap

      • Name clues: -pril suggests an ACE inhibitor, -sartan an ARB, -olol a beta blocker, and -statin a statin. Use the suffix as a hint, not as proof of the product, indication, or correct dose.
      • Duplication versus interaction: Two active NSAIDs may duplicate therapy. Warfarin plus an NSAID is a drug interaction that can increase bleeding, not a duplicate anticoagulant order. Both get flagged, but the reason differs.

      Check yourself: A profile lists Eliquis and a new apixaban prescription. Are they different drugs? No. They are the same active ingredient. Check for a brand/generic duplicate or a documented replacement rather than assuming both should be filled.

      Insulin and inhaler exam traps

      • Insulin: A prescription in units does not say how many mL are used unless the concentration is known. Match insulin name, concentration (such as U-100), device, and directions. Do not convert a pen dose into syringe markings on your own.
      • Inhalers: Check dose counter and labeled actuations. Rescue versus maintenance is a therapeutic distinction, not a universal rule about inhaler shape or color.

      Check yourself: Which would you flag if the patient thinks it is a rescue inhaler: albuterol or fluticasone alone? Fluticasone alone is the maintenance corticosteroid in this example; refer the misunderstanding to the pharmacist.

      04Medications: anti-infectives, mental health, pain, and interactions

      Learn one characteristic risk per drug or class. For drug-specific contraindications, interactions, and patient instructions, consult DailyMed.

      Generic (brand example) Class / typical use High-yield clue
      Amoxicillin (Amoxil) Penicillin antibiotic; susceptible bacterial infections Flag a reported penicillin allergy
      Amoxicillin/clavulanate (Augmentin) Penicillin plus beta-lactamase inhibitor Some ratios differ; do not substitute by amoxicillin mg alone
      Cephalexin (Keflex) Cephalosporin antibiotic Clarify the allergy/reaction history with the pharmacist
      Azithromycin (Zithromax) Macrolide antibiotic QT-prolongation risk in susceptible patients
      Doxycycline (Vibramycin) Tetracycline antibiotic Iron and certain antacids can reduce absorption
      Ciprofloxacin (Cipro) Fluoroquinolone antibiotic Tendon injury warning; interacts with some mineral products
      Sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (Bactrim) Combination antibiotic Flag sulfonamide antibiotic allergy or concurrent warfarin
      Sertraline (Zoloft) / escitalopram (Lexapro) SSRIs; depression/anxiety indications vary Check for serotonergic interactions, including with MAOIs
      Bupropion (Wellbutrin) Antidepressant; other products used for smoking cessation Different release forms; seizure risk is an important clue
      Alprazolam (Xanax) Benzodiazepine; anxiety/panic Combining with opioids increases respiratory-depression risk
      Oxycodone (Roxicodone, OxyContin ER) Opioid analgesic Check immediate versus extended release; respiratory depression
      Naloxone nasal (Narcan) Opioid antagonist; overdose reversal Different purpose and route from routine oral pain drugs
      Gabapentin (Neurontin) Anticonvulsant; also used for postherpetic neuralgia Drowsiness; review other sedating drugs

      Five ways an interaction can show up

      • Drug–drug: Nitroglycerin plus sildenafil can cause a dangerous drop in blood pressure.
      • Drug–supplement: St. John’s wort can interact with certain antidepressants and reduce the effect of other medications, including some oral contraceptives.
      • Drug–nutrient: Calcium or iron can reduce absorption of levothyroxine. The exact instructions should come from labeling and pharmacist review.
      • Drug–laboratory: Biotin supplements can interfere with some lab assays, including certain thyroid tests; flag the issue rather than interpreting the result (NIH Biotin Fact Sheet).
      • Drug–disease: Nonselective beta blockers such as propranolol may be contraindicated in asthma. A contraindication is a reason not to use a product in a defined situation, not merely a possible side effect.

      Check yourself: A patient says an antibiotic caused nausea. Should you independently relabel it as a true allergy or remove the entry? Neither. Record what happened accurately and ask the pharmacist to assess it.

      05Medications: forms, routes, reactions, stability, and storage

      Strength is how much drug is in a unit or volume; dose is how much the patient receives at one time. Duration is how long therapy continues. For example, a product labeled 250 mg/5 mL has that strength, while a 10 mL dose contains 500 mg; neither number alone says how many days treatment lasts (PTCE medication objectives).

      Match the form to the route

      • Oral versus mucosal: PO means by mouth; sublingual goes under the tongue; buccal goes between gum and cheek. An orally disintegrating tablet dissolves in the mouth but is not automatically a sublingual product.
      • Topical versus transdermal: A topical drug usually acts locally; a transdermal patch can deliver systemically. Product-specific instructions matter for patch placement, heat exposure, cutting, replacement, and disposal.
      • Release and coating: ER/XR/XL can signal extended release; DR or EC can signal delayed release or enteric coating. Do not assume a product can be crushed, split, or opened. Check the exact label or ask the pharmacist.
      • Local and injectable routes: Ophthalmic = eye; otic = ear. IM = intramuscular; subcutaneous = under the skin; IV = intravenous. Similar packaging is not proof of the correct route.

      Identify the reaction without diagnosing

      • Side effect: A known secondary effect, such as drowsiness with some antihistamines. Record what was reported instead of guessing whether it is benign.
      • Adverse effect: An unwanted or harmful drug effect, such as bleeding with an anticoagulant. Severity and next steps are for the pharmacist or emergency team to assess.
      • Possible allergy: Hives, facial swelling, throat swelling, wheezing, or breathing trouble can suggest an allergic reaction. Get immediate help for severe symptoms; follow emergency procedures rather than finishing routine data entry first.

      The stock-bottle expiration is not the only date

      • Expiration, beyond-use, and in-use dates: The manufacturer expiration goes with the unopened product stored as labeled. A reconstituted or opened product can have a shorter labeled discard interval. A pharmacy-assigned beyond-use date, when applicable, serves a different purpose from the manufacturer’s stock expiration.
      • Product specificity: Different antibiotic suspensions and insulin presentations can have different storage and discard periods. Read the label for the exact product, concentration, and device. Check light protection, freezing restrictions, and access or security rules as well as temperature (DailyMed).
      • Cold chain: A common refrigerated vaccine range is 2°C to 8°C, but use the actual product requirements. After a temperature excursion, label affected stock “DO NOT USE,” segregate it at the correct storage temperature, document the excursion, and seek guidance rather than dispensing or automatically discarding it (CDC Vaccine Storage and Handling Toolkit).

      Worked date problem: A fictional label states “discard 10 days after mixing.” If mixed November 4 at 9 a.m., ten elapsed days end November 14 at 9 a.m. Use the product instructions and site date-labeling policy for the actual discard label.

      06–08Federal Requirements

      06Federal requirements: schedules, fills, refills, and transfers

      On a federal-law question, first identify schedule, prescription type, and requested action. A refill, an initial fill at another pharmacy, and a partial fill are three different actions. This page summarizes federal rules; follow applicable state law and pharmacist direction in practice (DEA drug schedules; 21 CFR Part 1306).

      Schedule Common examples Federal study rule
      I Heroin, LSD No currently accepted medical use under federal scheduling criteria; not dispensed on a routine prescription
      II Oxycodone, morphine, methylphenidate No refills; normally a valid written or electronic prescription, with narrow exceptions
      III Buprenorphine, ketamine, certain codeine combinations No more than five authorized refills within six months of issue
      IV Alprazolam, diazepam, zolpidem, tramadol Same five-refill/six-month federal limit as Schedule III
      V Pregabalin, certain low-codeine cough products Do not automatically apply the federal III/IV limit; follow authorization and applicable law

      Translate the scenario into the right rule

      • Emergency C-II oral order: The federal emergency exception is limited to the quantity adequate for the emergency period, with a follow-up prescription from the prescriber within seven days. This is a pharmacist-handled exception, not a routine telephone refill (21 CFR § 1306.11).
      • Initial electronic C-II through C-V transfer: Federal rules permit a one-time transfer between retail pharmacies at the patient’s request, when state or other applicable law permits. It must stay electronic, without altering the prescription, and be communicated directly between licensed pharmacists (21 CFR § 1306.08).
      • C-III through C-V refill transfer: Generally a one-time transfer between pharmacies; pharmacies sharing a real-time online database may transfer up to authorized and legally permitted refills. Do not treat this as permission to refill a C-II (21 CFR § 1306.25).
      • C-II partial fill: An inability to supply the full amount generally has a 72-hour remainder window under the federal rule. Other scenarios have different rules, such as a qualifying patient- or prescriber-requested partial fill, generally within 30 days of the written date, with tighter limits for emergency oral prescriptions. Do not apply one deadline to all scenarios (21 CFR § 1306.13).

      Worked scenario: A C-IV order has five authorized refills and has already been refilled five times after the original fill. Two months remain inside the six-month window. No refills remain. The time and count limits both apply; neither overrides the other.

      07Federal requirements: controlled inventory, disposal, and hazardous waste

      Think in three stages: receive and secure → account and document → remove using the correct system. An inventory shortage, ordinary expired stock, and drugs returned by a household have different routes.

      Form/process What it is for
      DEA Form 222 or electronic CSOS equivalent Order/transfer Schedule I or II stock between appropriate registrants; C-II is the retail pharmacy example
      Invoices or equivalent records Document receipt of Schedule III–V stock; keep records retrievable as required
      DEA Form 106 Report theft or significant loss of controlled substances
      DEA Form 41 Record destruction when required; not the form for ordering or reporting theft

      Inventory and security

      • Initial and biennial inventory: Inventory when beginning covered activity and at least every two years thereafter; federal controlled records generally must be kept for at least two years. Count Schedule II exactly. For an opened Schedule III–V container, an estimate is generally allowed unless it holds more than 1,000 tablets or capsules, in which case an exact count is required (21 CFR Part 1304; § 1304.11).
      • Storage and dispensing: Federal rules allow pharmacy controlled stock in a securely locked, substantially constructed cabinet or dispersed among noncontrolled stock in a way that obstructs theft or diversion. Protect access, reconcile against receiving records, and use required prescription-label information such as patient, directions, and required warnings where applicable (21 CFR § 1301.76; 21 CFR Part 1306).
      • Theft or significant loss: Escalate promptly. The registrant gives written DEA notice within one business day of discovery and submits electronic Form 106 within 45 days of discovery (21 CFR § 1301.76).

      Don’t mix up disposal pathways

      • Pharmacy-owned controlled stock: Use authorized reverse distribution or another compliant registrant disposal pathway. Household take-back uses authorized collection channels; patient-returned controlled medications do not become pharmacy inventory (21 CFR Part 1317).
      • Hazardous drug versus hazardous waste: NIOSH’s hazardous-drug list addresses worker exposure; EPA rules determine the hazardous-waste stream, including applicable P-listed acute hazardous wastes. Use the designated container, required PPE, and site procedure. Do not pour hazardous waste pharmaceuticals into drains (NIOSH hazardous-drug list; EPA hazardous waste pharmaceutical regulations).
      • Nonhazardous pharmaceuticals: Keep them in the designated pharmaceutical waste or return stream according to classification and policy. Don’t assume “not controlled” means regular trash, sewer, or resale.
      08Federal requirements: pseudoephedrine, REMS, recalls, and DSCSA

      Each restriction solves a different problem. Pseudoephedrine rules control retail access; REMS addresses a specific drug’s serious risks; recalls remove or correct affected stock; DSCSA tracks covered products and protects the legitimate supply chain.

      Restricted products and programs

      • Pseudoephedrine: The federal limits are 3.6 g of base per purchaser per day and 9 g per 30 days at retail; a 7.5 g limit applies to the specified carrier/mail channel. Retail sales generally involve restricted access, ID verification, and a sales log. Stricter state rules may apply (DEA CMEA guide).
      • Worked limit check: A hypothetical product contains 30 mg of pseudoephedrine base per tablet. A 24-tablet pack contains 720 mg = 0.72 g of base. Six packs contain 4.32 g, above the 3.6 g federal daily limit. For actual labeled salts, use the system’s base-equivalent data, not the package’s salt strength as if it were base.
      • REMS: Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategies vary by drug. Depending on the program, check patient enrollment, prescriber or pharmacy certification, monitoring, dispensing authorization, and any quantity limit before proceeding (FDA Approved REMS database).

      Recall classes and the first action

      Class Meaning
      I Reasonable probability of serious adverse health consequences or death
      II Temporary or medically reversible consequences are possible, or the probability of serious harm is remote
      III Adverse health consequences are not likely

      Match the notice’s drug, strength, package or NDC, affected lot, and any expiration details. Stop dispensing matching stock, segregate and label it, alert the pharmacist, and follow the recall instructions for documentation, disposition, and any patient outreach (FDA drug recalls; 21 CFR § 7.3).

      DSCSA: trace it before releasing it

      • Receiving: Use authorized trading partners and required tracing information for covered drugs. The package identifier includes the NDC, serial number, lot number, and expiration date; retain required tracing records for six years (FDA dispenser requirements; FDA enhanced distribution security).
      • Suspect versus illegitimate: Quarantine and investigate suspect product; do not put it back into stock before it is cleared. After determining a product is illegitimate, the pharmacy must notify FDA and appropriate immediate trading partners within 24 hours (FDA DSCSA overview; FDA enhanced distribution security).
      • Current exemption note: FDA extended exemptions from certain enhanced requirements for qualifying small dispensers through November 27, 2027. That is not a blanket exemption from all DSCSA responsibilities (FDA exemption notices).

      09–10Patient Safety & Quality Assurance

      09Patient safety: prevent wrong-patient and wrong-product errors

      High-alert medications and look-alike/sound-alike drugs describe different risks. High-alert means an error can cause especially serious harm, as with insulin, opioids, and anticoagulants. LASA means names or packaging make a mix-up more likely. A medication can belong to both groups (FDA medication-error resources; ISMP communication guidelines).

      Use a check that matches the likely error

      Error What helps catch it
      Wrong patient Verify two identifiers, such as name and date of birth, against the actual record
      Wrong drug Compare the fully spelled name, strength, form, and order; verify the scanned product
      Wrong form or route Separate eye/ear and immediate/extended-release products; read the route before selecting
      Wrong dose Check units, decimal placement, concentration, and dose versus dose per day
      Wrong quantity Recalculate from dose and duration; compare to package size and intended days’ supply
      Wrong frequency Compare original Sig to written label; stop for an unusual or ambiguous frequency

      Layers of protection

      • Tall Man lettering: Capitalize different letters in confusing names, such as vinCRIStine versus vinBLASTine. Separate look-alike products and strengths; don’t rely on packaging color or shelf position alone (ISMP communication guidelines).
      • Barcode verification: Scan the actual product and respond to mismatches. A successful scan does not prove the original order or patient selection was correct.
      • Safe notation: Write 0.5 mg, not .5 mg, and 5 mg, not 5.0 mg. Spell out “units,” “daily,” and “every other day” instead of U, QD, and QOD; use mL, not cc (ISMP error-prone abbreviations).
      • Order that does not make sense: Never silently change it. Pause, preserve the original order, and refer the discrepancy to the pharmacist for clarification and documentation.

      Check yourself: What should you do with an order for oral methotrexate daily when the profile shows weekly dosing for a noncancer condition? Hold it and bring the frequency discrepancy to the pharmacist. A known prior frequency is a warning, not permission to edit the prescription.

      10Patient safety: escalation, reporting, and infection prevention

      On “what should the technician do first?” questions, address immediate danger, stop the unsafe process, and alert the pharmacist. Do not choose a substitute drug, recommend treatment, override a clinical alert, or decide on your own that an allergy does not matter (PTCE safety objectives).

      When the pharmacist needs to intervene

      • DUR: Drug utilization review checks issues such as interactions, therapeutic duplication, allergies, inappropriate doses, or early refill patterns. Gather accurate data and escalate the alert; don’t make an independent clinical override.
      • Adverse event or post-immunization concern: Alert the pharmacist; if there are signs of a medical emergency, activate the emergency response immediately. Document observations and timing without deciding that the drug or vaccine caused the event.
      • OTC choices, substitution, or adherence: Collect relevant facts for a pharmacist recommendation or substitution decision. Refill history can help reveal an adherence barrier but does not explain it on its own.
      • Suspected misuse or altered prescription: Don’t accuse the patient or process it as routine. Preserve information and follow the pharmacist’s and site’s procedures.

      Reporting and improving the system

      Term/channel What to remember
      Near miss Error occurred but was intercepted before it reached the patient; document per policy
      Error that reached the patient Alert the pharmacist promptly, even if no harm has been identified
      Adverse drug event (ADE) Harm involving medication use; can occur with or without a preventable error
      MedWatch FDA safety reporting for drug/device adverse events, quality issues, and appropriate medication errors (FDA MedWatch)
      VAERS Reports adverse events after vaccination; reporting does not establish causation (CDC/FDA VAERS)
      RCA and CQI Root-cause analysis asks why the system allowed the event; continuous quality improvement tests changes and tracks whether errors decrease

      Infection prevention

      Clean hands at required points, including before appropriate patient contact or aseptic tasks and after removing gloves. Wear PPE for the task and exposure risk; gloves do not replace hand hygiene. Clean shared work surfaces, counting trays, and equipment per policy, use the appropriate disinfectant contact time, and place used sharps in approved containers (CDC outpatient infection prevention guide).

      Check yourself: An incorrect-strength bottle was scanned, caught, and never reached the patient. Report it as a near miss per policy. Reviewing shelf layout, similar packaging, and interruption patterns helps prevent a repeat.

      11–14Order Entry & Processing

      11Order entry: prescription reading, Sig codes, and conversions

      A Sig is the directions for use. Read in this order: dose → route → frequency → duration → purpose or limits. Then match what you entered against the original prescription, including patient, drug, dosage form, strength, quantity and refills. If the order leaves out essential details, stop and have the pharmacist clarify it (PTCE order-entry objectives).

      Recognize abbreviations; write clear directions

      You may see Meaning You may see Meaning
      PO By mouth SL Sublingual
      BID Twice daily TID Three times daily
      QID Four times daily q6h Every 6 hours
      PRN As needed HS / qHS At bedtime in context; clarify ambiguous usage
      AC / PC Before / after meals STAT Immediately
      gtt Drop(s) OU / AU Both eyes / both ears; write out route
      tab / cap Tablet / capsule susp / soln Suspension / solution
      ii / v / x Roman numerals 2 / 5 / 10 NPO Nothing by mouth

      Decode this: ii caps PO BID x 7 days means “Take 2 capsules by mouth twice daily for 7 days.” Here x means “for” before the duration, while ii gives the dose. BID is not automatically the same as q12h; frequency and evenly spaced intervals convey different instructions. Avoid error-prone abbreviations in outgoing instructions (ISMP abbreviation list).

      Conversions to know

      Type Conversion
      Metric mass 1 kg = 1,000 g; 1 g = 1,000 mg; 1 mg = 1,000 mcg
      Metric volume 1 L = 1,000 mL
      Body mass 1 kg ≈ 2.2 lb; divide pounds by 2.2 to get kilograms
      Common pharmacy approximations 1 teaspoon ≈ 5 mL; 1 tablespoon ≈ 15 mL; 1 US fluid ounce ≈ 30 mL
      Temperature °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32; °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9

      Worked conversions: 0.125 mg × 1,000 mcg/mg = 125 mcg. A 44 lb patient weighs about 44 ÷ 2.2 = 20 kg. A refrigerator at 41°F is (41 − 32) × 5/9 = 5°C. A fluid ounce is volume, not weight; don’t confuse fl oz with oz.

      12Order entry: quantity, days’ supply, and number of packages

      For a fixed schedule, daily use = amount per dose × doses per day. Then quantity needed = daily use × days, or days’ supply = quantity dispensed ÷ daily use. Use the same unit for the numerator and denominator. When the problem asks for packages, round the number of whole packages up only if partial packages cannot be used and the question or policy calls for an intact package.

      Problem Work Answer
      2 tablets twice daily for 14 days 2 tabs/dose × 2 doses/day × 14 days 56 tablets
      150 mL dispensed; 5 mL three times daily 150 mL ÷ (5 mL × 3/day) 10 days
      1 tablet every 6 hours PRN, max 4 per day; 24 tablets At stated maximum: 24 ÷ 4/day 6 days at maximum use
      200-actuation inhaler; 2 puffs twice daily 200 ÷ (2 × 2) 50 days, if all 200 are usable for dosing in the question
      Taper: 4 tabs daily for 3 days, then 2 tabs daily for 4 days (4 × 3) + (2 × 4) 20 tablets over 7 days
      1 patch every 72 hours; 10 patches 72 hours ÷ 24 = 3 days per patch; 10 × 3 30 days

      Insulin, drops, and device-related quantities

      • Insulin example: A hypothetical carton has five 3 mL U-100 pens. Total = 5 × 3 mL × 100 units/mL = 1,500 units. At 30 units/day, ignoring priming and in-use discard for the exercise, supply is 50 days. A real package calculation must account for the actual pen instructions, required priming, package rules, and in-use expiration.
      • Eye-drop example: The question specifies 20 drops/mL. A 5 mL bottle contains 100 drops. One drop in both eyes twice daily uses 4 drops/day, so the supply is 25 days. Don’t assume one universal drop factor for every real bottle.
      • Intact packages: Need 140 mL; bottles are 60 mL and may not be split. 140 ÷ 60 = 2.33 bottles, so 3 bottles are needed. The total packaged quantity, 180 mL, is not the same as the calculated 140 mL therapy requirement.

      Exam trap: For a variable or PRN Sig, use a stated maximum when the question asks for maximum-use days’ supply. If the maximum or use rate is missing in practice, don’t invent it; follow pharmacy and payer policy or ask for clarification.

      13Order entry: dose, concentration, ratios, and dilutions

      Start with the unit you want. For a liquid, rewrite the available strength as mg/mL or set up desired dose ÷ available dose × available volume. For a tablet, divide the ordered mg by the mg per tablet; don’t assume a fractional tablet can be split if the formulation has not been verified.

      Work the units, not just the numbers

      Calculation Work Answer
      Ordered 375 mg; available 250 mg/5 mL 375 mg × 5 mL/250 mg 7.5 mL per dose
      Weight-based: 30 mg/kg/day in 3 doses; 44 lb; 100 mg/5 mL 44 ÷ 2.2 = 20 kg; 20 × 30 = 600 mg/day; ÷ 3 = 200 mg/dose; 200 × 5/100 10 mL per dose
      Ratio strength 1:1,000 w/v 1 g/1,000 mL = 1,000 mg/1,000 mL 1 mg/mL
      Percent strength 2% w/v 2 g/100 mL = 2,000 mg/100 mL 20 mg/mL
      Percent strength 2% w/w 2 g drug/100 g product; 30 g × 2/100 0.60 g (600 mg) drug in 30 g

      For w/v, percent means g per 100 mL. For w/w, it means g per 100 g. Do not substitute mass for volume unless the question gives you a way to convert them.

      Dilution with the same amount of dissolved drug

      Use C₁ × V₁ = C₂ × V₂ when diluting a stock with drug-free diluent. Use the same units for both concentrations, and make sure V₂ is the final volume, not the amount of diluent.

      Worked example: Prepare a hypothetical math solution of 120 mL at 5 mg/mL from a 20 mg/mL stock. V₁ = (5 × 120) ÷ 20 = 30 mL stock. Bring to 120 mL total; the simple additive-volume problem gives 90 mL diluent. Check: 30 × 20 = 600 mg and 120 × 5 = 600 mg.

      Rate calculation, when a problem gives time and a drop factor

      Worked example: 500 mL over 4 hours = 125 mL/hour. If the problem also gives 15 drops/mL, the gravity rate is (500 × 15) ÷ (4 × 60) = 31.25, or 31 drops/minute to the nearest whole drop. Solve the stated math only; actual preparation and administration follow approved procedures.

      Exam trap: First tell apart mg/kg/day from mg/kg/dose. Divide by frequency only when you started with a daily amount. Reconstitution also depends on the final labeled concentration; powder volume may make final volume different from diluent volume.

      14Order entry: supplies, NDCs, lot numbers, and returns

      Choose supplies that match the intended route and device. Use the actual product instructions and pharmacist guidance for compatibility or administration questions (PTCE equipment and inventory objectives).

      Supply Selection check
      Oral syringe Measure an oral liquid in mL; select a capacity that can accurately show the dose
      Insulin syringe/pen needle Match the exact insulin concentration and delivery system; do not use an oral syringe
      Blood glucose equipment Test strips must match the meter; distinguish testing supplies from administration supplies
      Inhaler spacer Match compatibility with the metered-dose inhaler; not a routine attachment for dry-powder inhalers
      Nebulizer Verify compatible medication solution and equipment, including tubing, cup, and mask or mouthpiece
      Injectable/immunization supplies Verify product, route-specific syringe/needle, required diluent or filter if specified, and safe sharps disposal

      Identify what the numbers actually tell you

      • NDC: Identifies labeler, product (such as strength and dosage form), and package. For ordinary 10-to-11 digit reimbursement conversion, use the dashed segments and add one leading zero to the short segment to make 5–4–2 (FDA NDC background; Michigan NDC conversion guide).
      • Worked NDC conversions: 1234-5678-90 → 01234-5678-90; 12345-678-90 → 12345-0678-90; 12345-6789-0 → 12345-6789-00. Remove dashes only after padding the correct segment. FDA’s future uniform 12-digit format takes effect March 7, 2033 and does not itself change the 11-digit reimbursement standard (FDA NDC format rule).
      • Lot, serial, and expiry: The lot identifies a production batch; the serial identifies an individual package in context; the expiration specifies the labeled shelf life under proper conditions. Same NDC does not mean same lot.

      Decide where the product can go

      • Return to stock: An eligible, never-picked-up fill may return under site policy if identity, integrity, storage, dating, and traceability are intact. Reverse the claim and update inventory as required. Do not mix unidentified tablets into a stock bottle.
      • Credit return or reverse distribution: Eligible stock may go through an authorized supplier or returns partner. Treat controlled returns through the separate compliant process, and don’t send quarantined product back without following the proper disposition instructions.
      • Nondispensable: Expired, compromised, recalled, or suspect stock does not go back on the dispensing shelf. Separate and document it. Medication already in a patient’s possession is not routine return-to-stock inventory.

      Do this without notes. Allow about 12–15 minutes, write units next to every calculation, and explain why the safest action in each scenario is safer than the tempting shortcut.

      Practice questions

      1. A patient has Eliquis on the profile and brings in a new prescription for apixaban. What safety issue should you check?
      2. A prescriber orders a liquid dose of 325 mg. The bottle is 250 mg/5 mL. How many mL are in one dose?
      3. You dispense 90 tablets with directions to take 1½ tablets twice a day. What is the days’ supply?
      4. A 66 lb patient has an order for 15 mg/kg/day in three equal doses. What is the dose in mg each time?
      5. How much 40 mg/mL stock is needed for 80 mL of a 10 mg/mL final solution?
      6. A Schedule IV prescription has already been refilled five times, but it is only four months old. Can it be refilled again under federal law?
      7. Does the federal rule absolutely prohibit transferring an unfilled electronic C-II prescription to another retail pharmacy?
      8. The labeled lot of a bottle matches a recall notice. What is the first inventory action?
      9. A controlled drug theft is discovered. Which DEA form applies, and what are the federal notification and filing windows?
      10. A patient reports an adverse event after vaccination. Which national system is specific to vaccines?
      11. Convert 54321-987-10 to the standard 11-digit reimbursement format.
      12. A 10 mL bottle is stated to contain 20 drops/mL. The Sig is 1 drop in each eye twice daily. How long does it last?

      Answer key and why

      1. Potential duplicate therapy. Eliquis is apixaban; determine whether the new order replaces an earlier one and refer the concern.
      2. 6.5 mL. 325 mg × 5 mL/250 mg = 6.5 mL.
      3. 30 days. 1.5 tablets twice daily = 3/day; 90 ÷ 3 = 30.
      4. 150 mg/dose. 66 ÷ 2.2 = 30 kg; 30 × 15 = 450 mg/day; divide by 3 doses.
      5. 20 mL stock. (10 × 80) ÷ 40 = 20 mL; bring to 80 mL final.
      6. No. The C-III/IV federal refill limit is five authorized refills within six months, not whichever limit allows more.
      7. No. A one-time initial electronic transfer may be allowed under the conditions on page 06; that is not a refill.
      8. Stop dispensing and segregate the affected stock. Notify the pharmacist and follow the recall procedure.
      9. Form 106. Written DEA field-office notice within one business day of discovery; electronic Form 106 within 45 days.
      10. VAERS. Follow local escalation and reporting procedures; reporting does not prove causation.
      11. 54321-0987-10. The middle (product) segment needed one leading zero.
      12. 50 days. 10 × 20 = 200 drops; two eyes × twice daily = 4 drops/day.
